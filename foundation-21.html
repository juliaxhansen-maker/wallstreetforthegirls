The Spread: Why Two Borrowers Pay Different Rates.

One idea, explained from zero, before the news starts.

Imagine two friends each asking to borrow $100 from the same bank, on the same day. One has a steady job, some savings, and a record of paying back on time. The other has a patchy record, a lot of existing debt, and a new promise to fix things. Same request, same bank. Should they get the same interest rate?

Of course not. The bank charges the steadier friend less, because she is less likely to fail to pay. The gap between the two rates is, in a sense, the price of doubt.

Countries borrow the same way. When a government needs money, it sells bonds: IOUs that pay interest and return the original amount on a set date. Each bond has a yield, the return an investor earns for lending at today’s price and holding until the bond ends.

Germany is treated as one of the safest large borrowers in the euro area, so its 10-year bond yield works as the benchmark. The sovereign spread is the extra yield another country pays above that benchmark for a loan of the same length.

How the number is read

Spreads are measured in basis points. One basis point is 0.01 percentage points, so 100 basis points is one full percentage point. On October 30, 2026, France’s 10-year yield was 4.95% and Germany’s was 3.35%. That is a spread of 160 basis points: investors were asking France for 1.6 percentage points more to hold a loan of similar length.

What the spread does and doesn’t tell you

A wider spread means investors want a bigger premium to hold that country’s debt. It reflects their view of its budgets, its politics, its growth, and the risk that it will not repay in full.

It does not mean a default is coming. France is still rated A+ by Fitch and S&P and still borrows in very large amounts. A spread is a price, and prices move on doubt before anyone knows the outcome.

Why it moves

The spread moves when the doubt does. Budget slippage, a surprise election, a change in a rating outlook, or a central bank decision can all push investors to ask for more, or for less. France’s spread widened this autumn as budget pledges kept slipping and the 2027 plan looked hard to deliver.

Your own version

Every time a lender asks for a bigger deposit because your record looks shaky, the same mechanism is at work. They are not passing judgment on who you are. They are pricing in what they have seen so far, and the price comes down as the record improves.

“If your next promise were priced like your last ten, what would it cost you?”

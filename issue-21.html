The Doubt Premium.

When France’s borrowing costs rise, the gap with Germany tells a bigger story about confidence, politics, and risk.

Disclaimer: Market figures are taken from the dates and observation times stated, with the snapshot using October 30, 2026, the last trading day of the month.

We all have that one friend who loves to shop. She has a stable job and a closet that somehow keeps getting fuller. Every year, she promises a no-buy year. A few months later, another shopping haul appears on Snapchat. And apparently, everything is an “investment piece.” She buys most of it through buy now, pay later, splitting each bill into smaller payments. She always pays, but not always on time.

The next January, she makes another budget. Then she finds tickets for Bryson Tiller’s Neo Trapsoul tour, and of course she can’t miss it.

She taps pay later and the app approves her. Only this time, the terms are tighter: a smaller limit or a bigger upfront payment.

Nothing about her salary changed. It’s just that the lender has heard this promise before.

France is that shopper this October.

On October 1st, France presented its 2027 budget. Bond investors kept lending but demanded higher yields. Germany still enjoyed lower borrowing costs. And Italy, despite having more debt and lower credit ratings, was borrowing more cheaply than France.[6]

The extra yield investors demand on French 10-year (10Y) bonds compared with German ones is called the spread. It’s measured in basis points, where 100 basis points equal one percentage point. A wider spread means investors demand a bigger premium to hold French debt, reflecting concerns about its finances and other market risks.

So why was France getting worse terms than Italy? Let’s go back a little.

The set up

For a long time, France got close to Germany’s terms. Its 10Y bond yield was often only around 60 basis points (0.6 percentage points) higher than Germany’s.[1]

Then came the first warning sign in June 2024, when a surprise early election left France with a divided parliament, making budgets harder to pass. Since then, two governments have fallen, and budget negotiations have dragged on.[2]

And just like her no-buy years, France’s budget pledges keep slipping. Its 2026 deficit target was 5% of GDP, but the government now expects 5.4%.[2][4] Public debt reached around 119% of GDP in Q2 2026.[5] For our shopper, that’s like owing more than she earns in a year.

The turning point

On September 18th, the French spread reached one full percentage point, or 100 basis points.[7] That was up from its historical level of around 60 basis points.[1] By September 30th, it had passed 120 basis points.[7]

Earlier in September, the European Central Bank (ECB) had raised its deposit rate from 2.25% to 2.50% to fight inflation. Asked about widening spreads, President Christine Lagarde declined to comment specifically on them.[8][44]

Then came the new pledge. On October 1st, the government presented its 2027 budget, aiming to bring the deficit from about 5.4% back to 5% of GDP. Il Sole 24 Ore noted that many of its measures could be largely reversed by whoever wins the 2027 election.[4] It was a no-buy year that her future self should cancel.

The market’s response was clear. On October 2nd, Germany’s 10-year yield fell to 3.41%, while France’s stood at 4.94%.[10] The difference was 153 basis points. And because German yields were falling while French yields stayed high, the spread was widening from both ends. Even though euro-area inflation came in hotter than expected at 3.8%, German yields fell, suggesting investors were seeking safety.[9][10]

Four days later, France was quoted at 140 basis points over Germany. Italy was at 116. Spain was at 64.[11] The friend with the bigger bill was getting the better rate.

On October 21st, France’s 10-year yield touched 5% for the first time since 2002.[29] The next day it climbed to 5.14%, and the gap with Germany was the widest since 2011.[28]

Then came the scorecard.

Fitch and S&P already rated France A+, and some analysts expected Moody’s to lower its Aa3 rating to A1 on October 23rd.[12][13] Instead, Moody’s surprised the market. It held at Aa3 and kept a negative outlook.[2][13] Approved again, with the terms still under review. Bond yields eased after the decision, offering France a brief moment of relief.[28]

Meanwhile, the ECB held its deposit rate at 2.50% on October 29th, as expected.[30] Lagarde had already made clear that the solution to France’s bond-market pressures lay in its own fiscal policy.[30]

But the relief was short-lived. On October 30th, Fitch kept France at A+ but changed its outlook to negative, warning that political divisions made lasting deficit reduction harder. It expects debt to reach around 124% of GDP by 2028.[41]

What to watch

Lenders are waiting for receipts. November 17th is a key deadline for France’s 2027 budget to move beyond the National Assembly.[18] S&P reviews France’s credit rating on November 27th.[13] The ECB’s next rate decision comes on December 17th.[19][50]

None of these dates tells us where the spread goes. But each gives investors another reason to reassess France’s borrowing risks.

The month in markets

October was a month of contrasts. French borrowing costs climbed while German yields fell. The euro weakened, but European stocks finished the month higher.

What moved

  1. France’s borrowing costs crossed 5%. France’s 10Y yield hit 5% for the first time since 2002, reaching 5.14% on October 22nd.[28][29] France now faces higher 10Y borrowing yields than Italy and Spain, despite Italy carrying more debt. France’s next budget and rating decisions will test whether that premium persists.[18]
  2. Investors looked for safety. On October 2nd, Germany’s 10Y yield fell to 3.41% as investors favored safer bonds. The French-German spread widened from both ends, with lower German yields and persistently higher French yields. The open question is whether Italy and Spain face similar pressure, or whether France remains the main concern.
  3. Inflation pressured the euro, even as stocks rose. Euro-area inflation reached 3.8% in September, with energy prices up 18.9% year on year.[25][45] The euro fell 1.61% against the dollar in October, ending the month near $1.12, while the STOXX 600 recorded its best month since June.[35][36] Persistent inflation complicated the ECB’s outlook, and currency and equity markets reacted differently to Europe’s economic pressures. The open question is whether inflation stays high enough to justify another ECB rate increase.

Who feels it first?

A wider spread isn’t just a number on a trading screen. When French yields rise, existing bonds generally lose market value, while new government borrowing becomes more expensive. Investors can feel the impact immediately, but for France, the costs build gradually as old debt is refinanced.

My take

What I am worried about isn’t just the rising borrowing costs in France. It is the possibility that the pressure could spread beyond government bonds and into the European banking system.

At least for now, the problem seems to be relatively contained. France’s spread reached around 160 bps on October 30th, while Italy and Spain remained much lower. But what happens if investors start demanding higher returns to lend to other European banks too?

Higher banking funding costs could mean more expensive loans and stricter lending conditions for businesses and households, which eventually weighs on economic growth. That would also put the ECB in a difficult position: trying to control inflation while preventing financial stress from disrupting monetary policy across the euro area.

I don’t think we are at that point yet. European banks seem resilient enough to absorb the pressure, and a wider French spread alone doesn’t necessarily mean a banking crisis is coming.

But I still wouldn’t ignore the risk.

Over the next one to six months, I’d watch sovereign spreads, bank funding costs and lending conditions together. If spreads keep widening, funding costs rise, banks’ net interest income comes under pressure, and lending conditions tighten, I’d become more concerned and reassess portfolio exposures.

But if funding costs stay stable, banks are still lending normally and inflation starts coming down, I wouldn’t be too worried.

At the end of the day, I think it’s better to be prepared and understand how these risks could affect your investments than to wait until things get serious.

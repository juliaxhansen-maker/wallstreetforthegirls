The Price of Borrowing Just Hit a Level Our Parents Remember

MAIN SOURCE: cnbc.com

FULL LIST OF REFERENCES IS AT THE END

Disclaimer: This issue was written on September 25th, 2026. Friday's Treasury figures are from CNBC's first report on Friday morning, not closing levels. CNBC updated its story later in the day.

Here’s what you need to know this week, and why it matters.

Every family has that one story.

It usually comes out at dinner, somewhere between the main course and dessert. Someone mentions a friend buying an apartment, or how much a new car costs these days, and one of our parents leans back in their chair and says, "You girls have no idea. When we bought our first place..."

Then comes the number. The interest rate they paid on that first mortgage, said slowly, with a small shake of the head, like they still can’t believe it. Sometimes there is a second part to the story too, about sitting across a desk from a bank manager who made them feel lucky to get the loan at all. And in that economy, maybe they were.

We have heard the story so many times that at this point we could tell it ourselves. And for most of our adult lives, it has sounded like a story from another world. Borrowing was cheap for a long stretch, and it felt like it always would be. The number in their story felt like history, the financial version of an old photo of them with a perm. Fun to look at, but not something we expected to see again.

Until this week.

The cost of borrowing money for the long term rose to a level last seen in June 2007. Many of us were still in school but our parents remember it like it was yesterday.

The rate in question comes from the US government bond market. When the US government needs money, it borrows by selling debt known as Treasuries. Buying one means lending the government money for a specified length of time, anywhere from a few weeks to thirty years. In return, the lender earns a yield, the yearly return they earn if they buy at today's price and hold the loan until it ends. You can think of the yield as the price of borrowing, seen from the lender's side of the desk.

The reason this matters far beyond America is that the US government is treated as the safest borrower there is. Many other loans are priced as a step up from it. The 10-year Treasury yield, the rate on a 10-year loan to the US government, is closely tied to what Americans pay on their mortgages. Banks, companies and governments everywhere watch it as their reference point. So when lenders demand a higher return from the US government, borrowing tends to get more expensive for everyone else soon after.

On Thursday, the 10-year yield reached 5.223%, its highest level since June 2007. The 30-year yield, touched 5.501%, a level not seen since June 2004. In CNBC's first report on Friday morning, both were still edging up from Thursday's close. The 10-year was up more than 2 basis points at 5.188%, and the 30-year was up more than 2 basis points at 5.49%. That may sound like nothing but across trillions of dollars of debt, it is a lot of money.

You might be wondering how we got here so quickly. Rates don't return to the level of our parents' dinner table stories by accident. Well this week, a handful of pieces of news landed within days of each other, and they all pointed the same way.

It started with the Federal Reserve. On September 16, it raised that rate by a quarter of a percentage point, to a range of 3.75% to 4.00%. It was the Fed's first rate hike since 2023, and all twelve voting members agreed. Afterwards, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh didn't soften the message. Asked whether more hikes would follow, he said, "I'm not in the forward guidance business." Forward guidance is when a central bank signals where rates are likely to go. By refusing to give any, he left the door open for more.

Then came Wednesday, and a survey. The S&P Global flash purchasing managers' index, or PMI, is an early monthly survey that asks businesses how busy they are. It showed US business activity growing at its fastest pace in years. It also showed the costs businesses pay for their own materials and services rising at the steepest rate since October 2022. An economy running that hot, with costs climbing that fast, is exactly what a central bank worries about.

The same day, Fed Governor Michael Barr, speaking at a housing affordability summit in Chicago, said: "In my base case, further policy adjustments are likely to be needed to ensure inflation comes down to target in a timely fashion." Barr had voted for the hike a week earlier, so markets read "further policy adjustments" as more hikes. A sitting Fed Governor was signalling that one rise might not be enough.

And then, that afternoon, came the part of the week that sounds most like our parents' story about the bank manager.

The US government regularly holds auctions, sales where it offers new debt to lenders and lets demand set the price. On Wednesday it sold five-year loans, and the lenders were noticeably less keen than usual. The debt sold at a yield of 5.033%. One important group of buyers, called indirect bidders, which includes foreign central banks, took just 54% of the sale against a usual 65%.

When lenders are lining up, borrowing is easy. When fewer of them show up, the borrower has to accept a higher yield to sell all of it. On Wednesday, even the US government had to raise its price to borrow.

On top of all this, Brent crude stayed above $100 a barrel. Expensive energy feeds into the price of almost everything else, which keeps the fear of inflation alive. By Friday, traders were pricing a 66% chance that the Fed raises rates again at its next meeting in late October, according to the CME FedWatch tool.

This isn't only America's story, either. Yields on Japanese, UK, German and other eurozone government bonds also hit fresh highs this week. Borrowing is getting more expensive for governments around the world at the same time.

But Friday looked different, though.

In Friday's early trading, the 2-year yield didn't move at all. It sat at 4.895% while the 10-year and 30-year edged higher. The 2-year is the loan length that follows the Fed most closely, and it had already done its running earlier in the week, reaching 4.941% on Thursday.

So by Friday morning, the part of the market that watches the Fed seemed to feel it had caught up. What kept rising was the long end, the loans that last ten and thirty years.

Padhraic Garvey, ING's regional head of research for the Americas, and Benjamin Schroeder, the bank's senior rates strategist, offered their view in a note on Friday. They wrote that there are already "enough rate hike fears discounted at this juncture," meaning markets have mostly priced in the hikes they expect. But they added that government bond yields "are primed to remain under pressure on a pure debt dynamic theory." One way to read that: the US government, like many others, is borrowing enormous amounts of money, and lenders asked to hold that debt for decades want to be paid more for it.

That's the part of our parents' story we never paid much attention to is the difference between borrowing for a little while and borrowing for most of our lives.

The 10-year is the yield American mortgage rates are tied to, so when it climbs, home loans tend to get more expensive. The same pressure tends to flow into car loans, business loans and the borrowing costs of governments everywhere. The stock market felt it too, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average falling for a third straight session on Thursday as high yields weighed on the market.

There is another side to this, and it matters just as much. Higher yields also mean anyone buying new long-term bonds is being offered more than in almost two decades. Savings account rates follow the Fed's short-term rate instead, which has only just started rising again. The same number that makes a mortgage more expensive makes a new bond more rewarding. It just depends on whether we're borrowing or lending.

So the number from the dinner table isn't only our parents' story anymore.

✦ The Signal

The Fed raised rates on September 16. A week later, Governor Barr said, "further policy adjustments are likely to be needed," and by Friday morning markets priced a 66% chance of another hike in October. The question now is how many more.

The 2-year jumped midweek, while the 10-year and 30-year hit their highest levels since 2007 and 2004. Wednesday's five-year auction had to clear at a higher yield than the market expected, with fewer indirect bidders than usual. When the world's safest borrower has to sweeten its terms, that tells us something about how lenders are feeling.

It's global. Japanese, UK, German and other eurozone government bond yields hit highs in the same week. When every major bond market moves together, the cause is bigger than any single country's news.

✦ The Noise

Friday's on its own move is small. The story is the level these yields reached this week.

Durable goods were a minor beat. Orders for long-lasting items like machinery came in roughly unchanged in August, against an expected 0.3% decline. It adds a little to the picture of a resilient economy, but it isn't what moved the market.

Friday's dip in Europe and Japan is one day of relief. Eurozone and Japanese yields edged lower on Friday, but one quieter session doesn't undo a week of fresh highs.

What’s moving in markets

Note: This week's article covers the US bond market. Figures for stocks and oil come from other reporting, cited at the end. The views on gold and company borrowing are informed context, not live figures.

Bonds. The 10-year yield hit 5.223% on Thursday, its highest since 2007, and the 30-year reached 5.501%, its highest since 2004. Both edged up again in early Friday trading. The 2-year held at 4.895% after its big midweek moves. Yields hit fresh highs in Japan, the UK and the eurozone too, leaving investors few calm markets to move money into.

Stocks. The Dow fell for a third straight session on Thursday. When safe bonds pay over 5%, investors want more from riskier assets, and share prices adjust.

Company borrowing. Business loans are priced on top of government yields, so when yields rise, companies' costs rise with them.

Oil. Brent settled Friday at $104.32, slightly up on the week. WTI fell about 8% to $92.41 on fears of a US diesel export ban, which would cut American refiners' demand for crude. For inflation, Brent above $100 is what counts.

Gold. [Weekly move]. It pays no interest, so higher yields make it more costly to hold.

Currency & interest rate

Where currencies stand

Note: This week's article does not cover currencies. The currency view below is informed context.

USD: Firmer bias. A renewed hiking cycle and a 10-year yield above 5% make the dollar more attractive to hold. Yields are rising elsewhere too, though, so the dollar isn't the only currency paying more, which softens the effect.

EUR/USD: Mixed. Higher German yields support the euro, while further US hikes pull money toward the dollar. The two roughly cancel out.

EM FX: Under pressure. Emerging market currencies tend to struggle when US yields rise, as money moves toward higher-paying dollar assets.

Key FX signal to watch: Whether the dollar keeps strengthening with US yields, or stalls because the sell-off is global and the gap with other countries isn't widening.

The rate picture

US 10-year: Up on the week. At 5.188% in early Friday trading, just below Thursday's 5.223%, the highest since June 2007.

Fed: Hiking. Rates sit at 3.75% to 4.00%, with a 66% chance of another hike priced for October.

ECB and BOE: Same pressure. Neither features in this week's reporting, but German and UK yields at highs show Europe is feeling the same forces, whether or not its central banks move.

Yield curve: Steeper. On Friday the long end rose while the 2-year held still. The gap between the 2-year and 10-year is about 29 basis points, and between the 2-year and 30-year about 60.

Key rate levels to watch: Whether the 10-year holds above 5% or breaks Thursday's 5.223% high, and what the Fed decides in October.

How it all connects

Inflation strand: Hot business survey + Brent above $100 → inflation fears → Barr signals more hikes → odds of an October hike rise → short-term yields jump

Supply strand: Heavy government borrowing → weak five-year auction → lenders demand more to lend long → long-term yields climb

Together: The 10-year hits its highest since 2007 → borrowing costs rise for governments, companies and households

The Fed only directly controls one end of this. It sets the shortest interest rate in the economy, and the 2-year follows it closely. The 10-year and 30-year are set by lenders deciding what they need to be paid to lock their money up for a decade or more. The Fed can influence that decision, but it can't make it for them.

So hikes only answer half the problem. They target inflation, and if they work, they may eventually calm the long end too, since lenders worry less about their money losing value over time. But the other half is the sheer amount of government borrowing, in America and around the world, and that's out of any central bank's hands. A rate hike doesn't make a lender more comfortable committing for thirty years to a borrower that keeps needing more.

You could see this on Friday: the short end sat still while the long end kept setting its own terms. And because the US government is the borrower everyone else is measured against, when it pays more to borrow long, everyone else does too.

Why this matters to you

The broader market picture

Markets are cautious, but nobody's panicking. Nothing collapsed this week, and there was no single dramatic day. What changed is the price of borrowing for a long time, now the highest in almost two decades. That feeds quietly into mortgages, business loans and the investments in our pensions.

If you are building your financial literacy

"Interest rates" is never just one number. There's the short-term rate the central bank sets, and the longer-term rates lenders set every day in the market. They usually move together, which is why we talk about "rates" as one thing. This week showed they don't have to, and why a headline about the Fed only tells half the story.

If this connects to your work or portfolio

  • Importers: If you pay for goods in dollars but earn in another currency, a firmer dollar makes them more expensive, and Brent above $100 keeps energy-linked costs high. WTI fell on the week, so which oil benchmark and which currency your contracts use decides how much of this reaches you.
  • Exporters: If you invoice in dollars but report in another currency, a firmer dollar means those payments convert into more of your own. For a US exporter, the same move makes goods pricier for foreign buyers. Either way, dollar strength built on expected Fed hikes can reverse, because expectations change faster than decisions.
  • Borrowers: Floating-rate debt tends to track the short end and the Fed. New long-term fixed loans, like a mortgage, tend to track the long end. This week those two ends moved differently, so knowing which one you're exposed to makes the headlines much easier to read.
  • Investors: Existing bonds lost value as yields rose, and the longest bonds lost the most. New money, meanwhile, is being offered the highest yields in years. The question worth asking is how much of what you own is sensitive to interest rates, and

Which of your plans were built on the assumption that borrowing would always stay cheap?

For much of our adult lives, money has been cheap to borrow, and it's easy to mistake that for how things always are. This week, the numbers from our parents' stories came back. The plans that hold up best, whether a mortgage you meant to refinance or a loan with a rate that moves, are the ones that still make sense if those numbers stay.
